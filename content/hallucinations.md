Ask an AI model for a source and it may hand you a perfect-looking citation for a paper that doesn't exist. It sounds sure. It's wrong. That's a **hallucination**.

## What a hallucination is

OpenAI defines hallucinations as "plausible but false statements generated by language models". Common forms:

- a made-up fact, date or number,
- a fake quote or citation,
- a function or API option that doesn't exist,
- a confident summary of a document that says something else.

The dangerous part is the tone. A hallucination reads exactly like a correct answer.

## Why it happens

Language models are trained to predict likely text, and then tuned and tested on how often they're right. OpenAI's 2025 research points to the incentive: "standard training and evaluation procedures reward guessing over acknowledging uncertainty."

Think of a multiple-choice exam. A guess might score a point. "I don't know" scores zero. A model graded that way learns to guess, and a guess, stated fluently, is a hallucination.

## How to cut them down

You can't remove hallucinations completely, but you can make them much rarer. Anthropic's guidance lists several techniques:

1. **Allow "I don't know".** Tell the model explicitly that admitting uncertainty is fine. Anthropic says this alone "can drastically reduce false information".
2. **Ground it in your documents.** Give the model the source material (that's what RAG does) and tell it to answer only from what's provided.
3. **Ask for direct quotes.** For long documents, have the model pull the exact quotes first, then answer from them.
4. **Require citations, and retract without them.** If the model can't find a supporting quote for a claim, it must drop the claim.
5. **Check the important stuff.** For anything that matters, verify against the source, or have a second pass review the answer.

## For developers

- Lower the stakes: show sources next to answers so users can check.
- For code, run it. Tests catch invented APIs fast.
- Measure it: keep a set of questions with known answers and track how often the model gets them wrong.

> Trust, but verify.
